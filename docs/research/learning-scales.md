# Learning scales on the piano: what teachers suggest

Research notes for a scale-exercise feature in onezan. Compiled 2026-11-27.
Notes per second (nps) figures are my own arithmetic from the published metronome marks;
everything else is cited. Where only a secondary source was available, the text says so.

## 1. Summary

Teachers, exam boards and method books agree on the core. Start with five-finger patterns
and one-octave scales, one hand at a time, slowly and legato, with a fixed standard fingering.
Add hands together and two octaves once the fingering is automatic. Bring in contrary motion
early, because the hands mirror each other and share a fingering. Add arpeggios and the key's
chords alongside each scale. Raise the tempo gradually with a metronome. Vary the practice
(rhythms, accents, dynamics, articulation) to make the playing even and controlled. Rotate
through all keys over a week instead of playing every scale every day.

All three boards checked (ABRSM, Trinity, RCM) follow the same arc. Their speed targets run
from about 2 notes per second at grade 1 to about 4.7–6 nps at grade 8 (8 nps for RCM level 10).
Most disagreement is about mechanics and order, not the overall arc:
- thumb-under against thumb-over or hand shift (Hanon against Chang; Ortmann/Leland say both);
- C major first against black-key scales first (Chopin, Chang);
- slow tempo ramps against speed bursts and parallel sets (Czerny against Chang);
- whether Hanon-style drill helps (Hanon for it, Chang strongly against).

An app can do the measurable half well: ordering, variants, a tempo ladder, and judging
evenness of timing and loudness from MIDI. It should leave hand shape, tension and tone
quality to a teacher.

## 2. Exam boards compared

Sources: ABRSM Piano Syllabus 2025 & 2026, pp. 13–14 and grade pages [A1]. ABRSM's 2027–28
syllabus keeps the same scale requirements [A2]. Trinity, *Piano Syllabus, graded exams from 2023*,
technical work pp. 27–45 [T1]. RCM, *Piano Syllabus, 2022 Edition*, technical tests per level [R1].

**How the tempo marks work.**
- ABRSM: all speeds "relate to the rhythmic grouping" of four beamed quavers. So a crotchet mark
  means 2 notes per beat, and a minim mark (grade 5 and up) means 4 notes per beat. ABRSM calls
  the speeds "a general guide" [A1 p.14].
- Trinity: a *minimum* pace, "increasing gradually grade by grade". All rhythms are "even quavers
  in pairs or fours", except the grade 1 triplet broken chords. Metronomes are not allowed in the
  exam [T1 p.27].
- RCM: the speeds are "a guideline for the minimum tempo". Each item lists its note value
  (quavers, triplets or semiquavers) [R1 p.7].

### Arpeggios (headline speeds)

- ABRSM [A1 p.14], same four-quaver grouping: Initial ♩=52, Gr 1 58, Gr 2 63, Gr 3 72, Gr 4 80,
  Gr 5 𝅗𝅥=44, Gr 6 50, Gr 7 56, Gr 8 66 (about 1.7 → 4.4 nps). They start at a 5th (Initial)
  or 1 octave HS (Gr 1). They reach 2 octaves HT at Gr 3–4 and 4 octaves HT with inversions
  at Gr 7–8.
- Trinity [T1]: min ♩=60 (Gr 2), 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, 120 (Gr 8), in quavers.
- RCM [R1]: tonic arpeggios begin at Level 4, HS ♩=72 in quavers. Level 8 is 4 octaves HT
  ♩=69 in semiquavers, and Level 10 is ♩=92 in semiquavers.

### Common features

- All three boards require scales from memory.
- All require ascending and descending.
- HT means the hands are an octave apart [A1 p.13; T1 p.27; R1 p.120].
- Each board starts with hands separately and moves to hands together at about grade 2–3.
- Each goes from 1 to 2 octaves early and to 4 octaves at grade 6.
- Contrary motion appears at the very first level of all three, as a hands-together task before
  similar-motion hands together.
- Staccato and dynamic contrasts come in at about grade 4–5. Thirds and sixths come at
  grade 6–8 (RCM: level 10).

### Other boards

- **AMEB (Australia):** I could not reach the official technical work requirements. They are

  unverified and not used here.
- **MTNA / US state syllabi:** not researched. They vary by state, and none was easily
  reachable as a primary document.

## 3. Method books and pedagogy literature

**Hanon, *The Virtuoso Pianist* (1873; Schirmer ed., tr. Baker)** [H1]
- Part II includes preparatory exercises "Turning the thumb under" the 2nd, 3rd, 4th and 5th
  fingers. Hanon calls the one under the 5th "of the highest importance".
- Then come the 12 major and 12 minor scales, each major followed by its relative minor, in
  harmonic and melodic forms. They are marked "M.M. ♩ = 60 to 120".
- Method: practise "with the metronome at 60 … and gradually increase the speed to 108",
  with fingers lifted "high and with precision".
- He claims the whole book takes an hour and should be played through daily. He presents it
  as "the key to all mechanical difficulties".
- Stance: thumb-under, slow-to-fast metronome ramp, daily drill.

**Czerny, *Letters to a Young Lady on the Art of Playing the Pianoforte*, Letter 2** [C1]
- Scales come first every day: "for one half hour, the first thing which you attack".
- Practise "in all the keys", right hand alone first and then both hands.
- Begin "extremely slow". Increase "from week to week" the degree of rapidity.
- Evenness has three parts: equal strength ("No one note ought to sound in the smallest degree
  louder than another"), equal speed, and equal duration ("No key must be held down for a
  longer or shorter time than the rest").
- This is the clearest primary statement of what "even" means, and it maps directly onto MIDI
  data: velocity, onset spacing and note length.

**Chopin, as reported by his pupil Mikuli** (secondary: quoted in *Etude*, 1905) [E1]
- Scales "with a full tone, as connectedly as possible, very slowly", then faster, with
  metronomic evenness.
- Order: B, F♯ and D♭ major first; C major last, "as the most difficult".
- Passing the thumb is eased by turning the hand inward.
- The original Mikuli preface and Eigeldinger's *Chopin: Pianist and Teacher* were not reached.

**Chang, *Fundamentals of Piano Practice* (free online)** [CH1, CH2, CH3]
- Advocates thumb-over (TO): the hand rolls over the thumb, with a glissando-like arm angle.
  "I recommend that you standardize to the TO method and use the TU as an exception."
- He keeps thumb-under for slow legato and held notes, and says "Both extreme schools are
  wrong because you need both skills" [CH1 §a].
- Start with B major, where the two thumbs are synchronised, not C [CH1 §c].
- Build speed with "parallel sets" (play 123, then 1234, as one quick motion) and cycling.
  "The fastest way to speed up scale playing is to practice only one octave."
- Master hands separately before hands together [CH1 §c].
- He criticises a pure slow-to-fast ramp, which he says builds a thumb-under habit that later
  has to be unlearned. Still, practise "most of the time at slower than maximum speed" [CH1].
- Scales and arpeggios are essential, practised hands separately [CH2 §d].
- On Hanon (§h), strongly negative: "essentially useless, or even harmful"; locked hands hide the
  weak hand; no rest built in; it takes time from repertoire.
- "Speed is skill, not strength"; practise softly; use bursts "briefly", as "an exception, not
  the rule" [CH2 §i].
- Recommends recording oneself: "You hear much more when listening to a recording than when
  playing it" [CH3].

**Alfred, *Scales, Chords, Arpeggios & Cadences* series (Palmer/Manus/Lethco)** [AL1]
(sample pages, Alfred cat. 00-22377, © 2004)
- Each key is presented as a two-octave scale, arpeggio and cadence (I–IV–I–V7–I in three
  positions).
- Practice suggestions, in order:
  1. legato HS, "listening for an even tone";
  2. crescendo up, diminuendo down;
  3. staccato;
  4. HT "when fingering is secure hands separately";
  5. RH *f* / LH *p*, then the reverse;
  6. RH legato / LH staccato, then the reverse;
  7. a rhythmic variant.
- Groups keys by fingering:
  - Group 1: C, G, D, A, E major (same fingering; 4th finger once per octave; thumbs on the tonic);
  - Group 2: F, B, F♯/G♭, D♭ (2–3 on the two-black-key group, 2–3–4 on the three-black-key group);
  - Group 3: B♭, E♭, A♭ (same LH fingering; RH 4 on B♭).

**Faber, *Piano Adventures Scale and Chord Books*** [FA1]
- Book 1: all major and minor five-finger scales and primary chords, for levels 1–2B.
- Book 2: one-octave scales and arpeggios, for levels 3A–3B.
- The core method "expands to two-octaves" at level 4.
- The Fabers reject "the obsolete tradition of mindless drilling". They favour pattern
  recognition (scale degrees, transposition) and "technical gestures for efficient and relaxed
  use of the wrist and body".
- Their editor suggests grouping the three minor forms together because the fingerings are
  so similar.
- Only the publisher's blog was reached, not the books.

**Leland, "The Ins and Outs, Twists and Turns of Scale Playing" (Piano Education Page)** [L1]
- Cites Otto Ortmann's research: "the thumb does indeed pass under the hand, but not
  necessarily all the way", and "the quick shift assumes more importance as the speed
  increases". So both camps describe part of the truth.
- Move the thumb as the 2nd finger plays, so the sideways move is separate from the keystroke.
- Isolated thumb drills come before full scales, starting with contrary-motion five-note
  figures.
- Notes that "virtually everyone starts with C Major" although Chopin did not.

**Not reached as primary text** (only lending-library scans or paywalled; nothing claimed from them):
- Neuhaus, *The Art of Piano Playing*;
- Gieseking & Leimer, *Piano Technique* [archive.org: `pianotechniqueco00leim`, access-restricted];
- Cooke, *Playing the Piano for Pleasure* [archive.org: `playingpianoforp00cook`, access-restricted];
- Fink, *Mastering Piano Technique*;
- Bernstein, *With Your Own Two Hands*;
- Jacobson/Faber, *Professional Piano Teaching*.

### Where they agree

- Start slowly; legato; full tone; hands separately first [C1, E1, CH1, AL1, S1].
- Fixed, standard fingering learned early. The 4th finger is the landmark [S2, AL1].
- Evenness of loudness, timing and duration is the goal [C1, E1, AL1].
- All keys eventually, with arpeggios and chords alongside [H1, AL1, all boards].

### Where they disagree

| Question | Position A | Position B |
|---|---|---|
| Thumb | Thumb-under, drilled [H1]; turn hand inward to help it [E1] | Thumb-over as the default for speed, thumb-under as the exception [CH1] |
| | Both, plus a lateral hand shift that grows with speed (Ortmann via Leland [L1]); Fitch teaches "blocking" both with an arm shift and with thumb-under [F2] | |
| First keys | C major first, then circle of fifths / white-key group (most method books, boards) [L1, AL1] | B / F♯ / D♭ first, C last [E1, CH1] |
| | Colourful Keys (teacher blog) lists the trade-offs and says to choose on purpose [K1] | |
| Getting faster | Slow metronome ramp: start at 60, raise gradually [H1, C1, S1] | Parallel-set bursts and one-octave cycling, briefly [CH1, CH2] |
| | Fitch: slow "chaining" first, later group in 4s and think of "the sweep of the arm"; finish with an optional as-fast-as-possible attempt [F2, F1] | |
| Hanon-style daily drill | Daily, full book [H1] | "Useless, or even harmful" [CH2]; Fabers reject "mindless drilling" [FA1] |
| HT | Both hands early (boards from grade 1–3) | Mostly HS; HT only briefly at first [CH1, CH2] |

## 4. Practice techniques and who recommends them

| Technique | What | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Five-finger patterns (pentascales) first | Tonic to dominant, no thumb crossing | RCM Prep A/B [R1]; Faber Book 1 [FA1]; Hoffman Academy (short mention only) [HA1] |
| Hands separate, then together | HT only once the fingering is secure | Czerny [C1]; Alfred [AL1]; Chang [CH1]; Spanswick [S1, S2]; Kovarsky ("the more dominant hand often covers up the weaker hand") [Y2] |
| Contrary motion early | Hands mirror each other and share a fingering; both thumbs act together | All three boards, first level [A1, T1, R1]; Leland's thumb drill [L1]; Pierce (five-finger patterns in contrary motion) [Y1] |
| Slow, full tone, then lighter | "Very slowly … full tone" | Chopin/Mikuli [E1]; Czerny [C1]; Spanswick ("very slow speeds using a full tone, then lighten the tone") [S2] |
| Metronome tempo ladder | Raise the tempo gradually ("from week to week") | Hanon 60→108/120 [H1]; Czerny [C1]; Spanswick [S1]; Fitch ("very good to do it with the metronome") [F1] |
| Subdivision ladder | Same beat, 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 notes per beat, 1 → 4 octaves | Fitch, after Nehama Patkin [F1]; Kovarsky (quavers → triplets → semiquavers with a click; practise the switch) [Y2] |
| Rhythmic variants | Dotted, triplet or other rhythm patterns, then back to even | Fitch [F2, F3]; Alfred [AL1]; Spanswick (triplets, dotted) [S2] |
| Accents and groupings | Accent in 3s, 4s or 6s, then remove the accents | Fitch [F3, F4]; Spanswick ("accentuation is probably one of the best" ways to coordination) [S2] |
| Blocking / thumb preparation | Play each hand position as a chord; move the thumb early and have it ready over its note | Fitch [F2, F5]; Leland [L1]; Pierce (thumb moves "as soon as it has played"; a late thumb makes a "bump") [Y1] |
| Bursts / parallel sets / chunking | Short groups at speed, linked; cycle one octave | Chang [CH1, CH2] |
| Dynamics | *f*, *p*, cresc. up / dim. down, then the reverse; hands at different dynamics | Alfred [AL1]; Kovarsky [Y2]; Fitch [F3]; Pierce [Y1]; Trinity requires it from grade 2 [T1] |
| Articulation | Legato, staccato, non-legato, leggiero; hands with different articulation | Alfred [AL1]; Spanswick [S2]; Kovarsky [Y2]; Fitch [F3]; boards from grade 4–5 |
| Hands at different speeds or offsets | One hand twice as fast; 2-against-3; hands 2 octaves apart, or a 3rd or 6th apart | Fitch [F3]; Spanswick [S2]; boards at grade 7–8 |
| Arpeggios and cadences alongside | Same key, same session | Alfred [AL1]; all boards; Spanswick [S1] |
| Rotation | 2–4 keys a day, in varied order, all keys over a week; circle of fifths or random order | Spanswick ("2 or 3 keys per day", "different order to that in the scale manual") [S2], ("3 or 4 keys per day in rotation") [S1]; Fitch (circle of fifths or random generator) [F3] |
| Daily length | Czerny: half an hour first thing [C1]; Hanon: an hour for the whole book [H1]; modern teachers give rotation rather than a length [S1, S2] | |
| Judge evenness by ear and recording | Listen to each hand; record and listen back | Chang [CH3]; Pierce [Y1]; Czerny (teacher or "your own ear") [C1] |
| Physical setup | Relaxed shoulders, curved fingers, wrist level, no elbow drop at thumb passing | Spanswick [S2]; Pierce [Y1]; Fitch [F5]. Needs a teacher's eye. |

## 5. The rounded approach (modelled)

This is a synthesis. **[C]** marks consensus across several independent sources. **[S]** marks
one school's view, named. Tempo marks follow the boards; nps is notes per second.

### Stage 0: pentascales (beginner, about RCM Prep A / ABRSM Initial)
- **Keys:** C, G, D, A major, A minor, then all major and minor five-finger patterns [C].
- **Hands:** HS, then HT in contrary motion and parallel.
- **Tempo:** ♩=60–100, one note per beat (about 1–1.7 nps).
- **How:** legato and staccato; listen for equal loudness.
- **Pass when:** it is steady at the target from memory.

### Stage 1: one octave, hands separately (about grade 1 / Level 1)
- **Keys** (default [C]): C, G, D, A, E major (one fingering), with the relative or parallel
  minors (natural, then harmonic). Then F, then B♭, E♭, A♭, then B, F♯/G♭, D♭.
- **Keys** (alternative [S], Chopin/Chang): B, F♯, D♭ first, for students who struggle with
  thumb crossing on white keys.
- **Hands:** HS. C-major contrary motion HT as the first hands-together scale [C, boards].
- **Before full scales** [C]: a thumb-preparation drill, then blocking (play each hand position
  as a chord).
- **Tempo:** start at ♩=40–60 in crotchets. Target ♩=60–70 in quavers, about 2–2.3 nps.
- **How:** legato, *mf*, full tone; correct fingering every time.
- **Pass when:** no fingering errors, and no thumb "bump" in loudness or timing.

### Stage 2: two octaves, hands together (about grade 2–3 / Level 2–3)
- **Keys:** add keys around the circle of fifths. Add harmonic and melodic minors [C].
- **Hands:** HT only after HS is secure [C].
- **Octaves:** 2. Add the chromatic scale (HS) and contrary motion over 2 octaves.
- **Arpeggios:** one key's arpeggio and its I–IV–V–I chords in the same session [C].
- **Tempo:** ♩=66–90 in quavers, about 2.2–3 nps.
- **Variants:** crescendo up and diminuendo down; *f* and *p* versions (Trinity requires this at
  grade 2).
- **Practice:** dotted and reverse-dotted rhythms, then even again [C].

### Stage 3: control (about grade 4–5 / Level 4–6)
- **Keys:** all major and minor keys.
- **Articulation:** legato and staccato HT.
- **Contrary motion:** in harder keys.
- **Chromatic:** HT, then in contrary motion.
- **Hands differ:** different dynamics per hand, different articulation per hand [AL1, F3].
- **Tempo:** ♩=100–110 in quavers (3.3–3.7 nps). At the top of the stage, 4 notes per beat at
  ♩=60 (4 nps).
- **Subdivision ladder** [S, Fitch/Patkin; Kovarsky]: 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 notes per beat on one
  metronome setting.
- **Accent groups:** 3s and 4s, then removed [C among the modern teachers].
- **Bursts** [S, Chang]: brief parallel-set bursts on the hardest junction.
- **Rotation:** 2–4 keys per day, in varied order [S1, S2].

### Stage 4: advanced (about grade 6–8 / Level 7–10)
- **Range:** 4 octaves HT, legato or staccato on request.
- **Dynamics:** cresc./dim. shapes (*p–f–p*).
- **Harder forms:** scales a 3rd or 6th apart; 3rds HS (slower: 𝅗𝅥=46–80); whole-tone; octaves.
- **Arpeggios:** inversions, dominant and diminished 7ths.
- **Tempo:** 4 notes per beat at ♩=76–88 (5–6 nps) by grade 8 / Level 8. RCM Level 10 asks
  ♩=120 (8 nps).
- **Thumb mechanics** at this speed: Chang says switch to thumb-over [S]. Ortmann/Leland say a
  lateral shift added to the thumb-under motion [S]. Either way, the motion becomes a continuous
  arm glide grouped in 4s, not finger-by-finger [F2, CH1].

### Throughout [C]
- From memory. Evenness is judged on three axes: loudness, timing, duration [C1].
- Record and listen back [CH3].
- Musical shaping, not only accuracy: ABRSM's top band wants "musically shaped" scales [A1 p.59].
- A full-speed attempt now and then, accepted as messy [F1].

## 6. Implications for onezan

What the app can do well:

- **Ordering:**
  - offer the C-G-D-A-E fingering group first, then F, the flat keys and the black-key group;
  - offer a "black keys first" option [S];
  - group related minors with each major;
  - offer a daily rotation of 2–4 keys, in a different order each day.
- **Stages as presets:** pentascale → 1 octave HS → contrary motion HT → 2 octaves HT →
  chromatic → 4 octaves. Defaults should follow the stage. Manual overrides can wait.
- **Tempo ladder:**
  - use the board figures as targets, about 2 nps at the start and 4–6 nps at the top;
  - step up a few BPM after a clean pass, down after a failed one;
  - keep the beat fixed and change the subdivision (2 → 3 → 4 per beat), since the app already
    sets the metronome.
- **Rhythm and accent variants:**
  - dotted, reverse-dotted and triplet versions of the same scale, then "back to even";
  - accent groups of 3 or 4, checked through velocity peaks on the expected notes.
- **Articulation and dynamic variants:**
  - staccato can be checked with note-off timing (duration ratio);
  - *f*/*p* and cresc./dim. can be checked with velocity trends;
  - hands at different dynamics can be checked with velocity per hand.
- **Evenness judging from MIDI** (Czerny's three equalities):
  - timing: the spread of inter-onset intervals, and drift against the click;
  - loudness: the spread of velocity, plus a specific check for a velocity or timing bump on
    thumb-crossing notes;
  - duration and legato: overlap or gap between consecutive notes;
  - HT: the onset offset between the hands on unison notes (a weaker hand "dragging").
- **Show results per position:** show where in the scale the unevenness sits (for example
  "every RH thumb is early and loud"), not just a score. This stands in for "record yourself
  and listen back".
- **Fingering:** the app shows standard fingering. It cannot see which finger played, but it
  can spot the timing and velocity bumps that wrong or late crossings typically cause.

What it should not try to judge:
- hand shape, wrist height, elbow drops, tension or pain;
- thumb-under against thumb-over technique;
- tone quality beyond velocity;
- whether a passage is "musically shaped" beyond the dynamic contour.

These need a teacher's eye and ear. The app can say so, and can suggest slowing down when
evenness collapses near the tempo limit, which is commonly a sign of tension. Do not present
Chang's thumb-over or Hanon-style daily drill as the one right way: both are single-school views.
